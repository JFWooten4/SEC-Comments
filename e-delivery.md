# Dossier: INVEST Act § 205 — *Improving Disclosure for Investors*

Your identification is correct. The SEC’s reference in **Question 13 of proposed Regulation E-Delivery, Release No. 33-11430**, is to **§ 205 of H.R. 3383**, the House-passed *INVEST Act of 2025*. Question 13 deliberately borrows the formulation “an electronic method reasonably designed to ensure receipt” and footnote 110 sends the reader back to footnote 46. Footnote 46 identifies **H.R. 3383 § 205** and expressly says, “This bill has not been enacted.” ([thefederalregister.org][1])

There is one useful refinement to the description: **H.R. 2441 is the immediate standalone source of § 205, but the legislation has a longer lineage going back to 2022.** And H.R. 3383 itself began life as a different, much narrower bill before becoming the vehicle for the INVEST Act package.

## How H.R. 3383 became the INVEST Act

This explains a potentially confusing documentary trail. **H.R. 3383 was originally the *Increasing Investor Opportunities Act*.** In December 2025, the House Rules Committee made **Rules Committee Print 119-15** the amendment in the nature of a substitute. That print combined more than twenty Financial Services bills, expressly including **H.R. 2441**, and gave the resulting package the short title *Incentivizing New Ventures and Economic Strength Through Capital Formation Act of 2025*, or **INVEST Act of 2025**. ([Rules Committee][4])

That is why the official House roll-call page still labels the Dec. 11 vote “Increasing Investor Opportunities Act,” while the version sent to the Senate is officially titled the *Incentivizing New Ventures and Economic Strength Through Capital Formation Act of 2025*. They are the same H.R. 3383 at different stages of amendment. ([Clerk of the House][5])

## What § 205 actually would do

Section 205 is considerably more than a definition. It directs the SEC, **within 180 days of enactment**, to propose changes allowing covered entities to satisfy securities-law document-delivery obligations electronically and requires finalization **within one year**. ([GovInfo][6])

Its model is effectively **paper-to-electronic conversion by default**, but with prescribed safeguards. An investor not already receiving everything electronically first receives a paper communication; there can then be a transition period of up to 180 days. For up to two years afterward, the investor receives an annual paper reminder that paper delivery remains available. Investors must be permitted to opt out of electronic delivery at any time. The SEC rules must also address failed electronic deliveries, readability and retainability. ([GovInfo][6])

A particularly consequential provision is the statutory backstop: **if the SEC fails to finish the required rulemaking within one year, covered entities may themselves use electronic delivery under the statutory conditions, and that delivery is deemed to satisfy the securities-law delivery obligation.** In other words, congressional inaction by the agency would not indefinitely prevent the new delivery regime from operating if § 205 became law. ([GovInfo][6])

It also exempts documents delivered under § 205 from **§ 101(c) of E-SIGN, 15 USC 7001(c)**, while stating that § 205 does not change the *substance or timing* of the underlying disclosure obligations. Separately, the SEC would have to review requirements for documents to be delivered “in writing,” and SROs would have to conform their rules. A federal statute that specifically requires paper is preserved. ([GovInfo][6])

## Transfer agents are expressly included

This is not a bill whose application to TAs has to be inferred. The definition of **“covered entity” expressly includes “a registered transfer agent” as defined in Exchange Act § 3(a)(25)**. ([GovInfo][6])

That matters in another provision as well. Section 205 requires additional confidentiality measures for covered entities **other than** brokers, dealers, SEC-registered investment advisers, and investment companies. Transfer agents are not within those exclusions, so a registered TA would fall within § 205(b)(7)'s requirement for measures reasonably designed to protect personal information in electronically delivered regulatory documents. ([GovInfo][6])

The statutory definition of regulatory documents is deliberately broad. It specifically names prospectuses, shareholder reports, certain notices, confirmations/account statements, proxy statements, Regulation S-P notices and Regulation S-AM notices, and then ends with a catch-all for **all other regulatory documents that covered entities must deliver to investors under the securities laws or SEC/SRO rules**. ([GovInfo][6])

## The definition the SEC is interested in

The operative definition is technology-neutral. “Electronic delivery” includes direct delivery to an investor's electronic address; posting the document to a website while directly notifying the investor at an electronic address; and:

> “any other electronic method reasonably designed to ensure receipt”

of the regulatory document by the investor. “Website” itself includes other electronic information repositories and specifically **mobile applications**. ([GovInfo][6])

That third category is exactly what caught the SEC's attention in **Question 13**. The Commission asks whether its own proposed definition should be broadened with language like § 205's “reasonably designed to ensure receipt” standard. The immediately following questions discuss web portals, mobile applications and then expressly ask whether the SEC definitions are technologically neutral enough to accommodate **blockchain or similar technology**. ([thefederalregister.org][1])

So § 205 is particularly relevant to a system that does not conceive of “electronic delivery” as synonymous with **email attachment**. Its text affirmatively contemplates electronic addresses, websites, mobile applications, and an open-ended third category.

## Relationship to the SEC's 2026 proposal

The distinction here is important: **the SEC is not claiming § 205 gives it statutory authority, because § 205 is not law.** It cites the legislation as congressional context for the policy question it is considering. Footnote 46 says the Commission is aware of “certain bipartisan congressional support” for default electronic delivery, names H.R. 3383 § 205, and immediately says the bill has not been enacted. ([thefederalregister.org][1])

Question 13 then effectively asks commenters whether the SEC should independently adopt one of the concepts Congress used in § 205. ([thefederalregister.org][1])

There is also a small but amusing drafting error in the SEC release: footnote 46 calls H.R. 3383 the **“Incentivizing New Adventures and Economic Strength Through Capital Formation Act”**. The actual statutory short title is **“Incentivizing New Ventures and Economic Strength Through Capital Formation Act of 2025.”** ([thefederalregister.org][1])

## The policy dispute around H.R. 2441

The House committee majority's report characterized default electronic delivery as potentially reducing paper use, making disclosures easier to search, and improving accessibility, while emphasizing the bill's paper opt-out. It cited a SIFMA survey reporting that 85% of surveyed individual investors would be comfortable with e-delivery as the default if paper remained available. That figure is a committee-cited industry survey, not an independent congressional measurement. 

The report's minority views argued that a default-electronic system could disadvantage investors without reliable internet access or those who prefer paper, particularly older investors, and objected to both the automatic transition and the self-effectuating provision if the SEC misses its deadline. The minority views cited opposition from AARP, NASAA, the Consumer Federation of America, Americans for Financial Reform and Public Citizen. 

Those are the two principal competing policy frames in the legislative history: **lower-cost/modernized delivery with a paper opt-out versus concern that an opt-out system may leave some investors less likely to receive or use disclosures.**

## Current status — September 17, 2026

The official H.R. 3383 record still lists **December 15, 2025** as its latest action: received in the Senate, read twice, and referred to the **Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs**. It therefore remains unenacted as of today. ([GovInfo][7])

The House-passed vote was **302–123** on December 11, with 8 not voting. ([Clerk of the House][5]) Separately, S. 1877's official record continues to show its May 22, 2025 introduction and referral to the same Senate committee. ([GovInfo][3])

For a compact reference in your SEC-comment notes, I would identify the source as:

**INVEST Act of 2025, H.R. 3383, 119th Cong. § 205 (House-passed Dec. 11, 2025) (unenacted), substantially incorporating H.R. 2441, the Improving Disclosure for Investors Act of 2025.**

That is more precise than merely saying “currently unenacted Congressional legislation,” while also distinguishing the **standalone bill genealogy** from the **House-passed package vehicle**.
